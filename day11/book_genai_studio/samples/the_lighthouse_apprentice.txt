Chapter 1: The Letter

Mira Okafor was fourteen when the letter arrived at the orphanage in Port Saltry. It was written in green ink, folded three times, and sealed with a blob of candle wax pressed flat by a thumb. The matron read it twice before handing it over, which Mira took as a bad sign, because the matron never read anything twice.

The letter was from Elias Brann, keeper of the Harrow Point lighthouse. He wrote that his eyes were failing, that the shipping company refused to send a replacement until spring, and that he needed an apprentice who could climb stairs, count, and stay awake. "Pay is room, board, and one silver coin each month," he wrote. "The work is lonely. The view is the best in the world."

Mira had never seen the sea up close, only the grey edge of it beyond the warehouses. She had, however, spent three winters keeping the orphanage stove alive through the night, and she knew how to stay awake. She also knew that nobody else in the building would answer a letter in green ink from a man who lived on a rock.

She packed her two dresses, her mother's brass compass, and a notebook with only four empty pages left. The matron gave her a loaf of bread and a lecture about manners. The coach left at dawn, and by evening Mira could smell salt in the air for the first time in her life.

Chapter 2: Harrow Point

Harrow Point was not an island, exactly, but it might as well have been. A narrow causeway of black stones connected it to the mainland, and twice a day the tide swallowed the causeway whole. Elias Brann met her at the far end with a lantern, although the sun had not yet set.

He was tall and bent, like a tree that had grown up against the wind. His beard was the colour of old rope, and he squinted at everything, including Mira. "You're smaller than I hoped," he said. "But the stairs don't care how big you are. Only how often you climb them."

The lighthouse had one hundred and twelve steps. Mira counted them on the first night, and again every night after, because Elias told her that a keeper who loses count of the steps will soon lose count of other things. At the top was the lamp room, where a great glass lens sat like a beehive made of ice. The lamp burned whale oil at first and later paraffin, and it had to be trimmed every four hours so the flame stayed tall and clean.

Her duties were simple to describe and hard to do. She cleaned soot from the lens each morning with soft cloths and spirit. She wound the clockwork that turned the lens, so the light flashed twice every ten seconds, which was the signal that told ships this was Harrow Point and not some other rock. She carried oil up the stairs in tin cans that bit into her fingers. And every night she sat in the watch room, staying awake, watching the dark water for ships in trouble.

Elias taught her the names of the winds and the meaning of the clouds. He was patient about mistakes and impatient about laziness. When she spilled oil on the stairs, he only handed her a rag. When she dozed off during her first watch, he did not speak to her for a whole day, and that silence taught her more than shouting would have.

Chapter 3: The Logbook

In the watch room there was a shelf of logbooks, forty years of them, bound in cracked leather. Every keeper of Harrow Point had written down the weather, the ships that passed, and anything unusual. Elias asked Mira to read them aloud in the evenings, since his eyes could no longer manage the small handwriting.

Most entries were dull: wind north-east, light rain, two fishing boats, lamp trimmed at midnight. But in the logbook from twenty-two years earlier, Mira found entries in a different hand, neat and slanted, signed with the initials A.O.

Her mother's name had been Adaeze Okafor.

Mira read the entries over and over. A.O. had been the assistant keeper for three years. She wrote about seabirds and storms, and once about a ship called the Corrigan that nearly struck the rocks because the clockwork had jammed. A.O. had turned the lens by hand for six hours until dawn, and the Corrigan had sailed safely past. The last entry by A.O. said only: "Leaving for Port Saltry. Child expected in spring. Will return."

She never returned. Mira had been told her mother died of fever when Mira was two.

When Mira finally asked Elias, he was quiet for a long time. Then he told her the truth: he had written to the orphanage because he had promised Adaeze, long ago, that if anything ever happened to her, he would look after her child. It had taken him twelve years to find out where the child had gone. "I should have looked harder," he said. "I'm sorry I was slow."

Mira did not know whether to be angry or grateful, so she was both, and then she went up the one hundred and twelve steps and trimmed the lamp, because it was time.

Chapter 4: The Storm

The great storm came in the last week of November. For two days the barometer fell, and Elias grew restless, tapping the glass as if he could argue with it. On the third evening the wind arrived like a wall. Rain struck the lamp room windows sideways, and the whole tower hummed.

Around midnight, the clockwork stopped.

Mira heard it before she understood it: the steady ticking that had been the heartbeat of every night simply ended. The lens stood still. Without its rotation, the light no longer flashed twice every ten seconds; it was just a fixed glow, and a ship's captain could easily mistake it for another lighthouse further down the coast and steer straight onto the rocks.

Elias tried to repair the mechanism, but his hands shook and his eyes could not see the broken spring. Through the rain, Mira saw the lights of a ship to the south, far too close to shore.

She remembered the logbook and her mother's six hours. She took hold of the brass handles on the lens carriage and pushed. It was heavier than anything she had ever moved. She found the rhythm by counting out loud, the way she counted the stairs: push for two flashes, wait, push again. Elias counted with her, his voice steady even though he could not help with his hands.

She turned the lens for five hours. Her palms blistered and then bled. Near dawn, the ship, a cargo steamer called the Wren, changed course, rounded the point, and found shelter in the bay. The captain later wrote to the shipping company that the Harrow Point light had been "true as a clock all night long."

Chapter 5: The New Keeper

In spring, the shipping company finally sent an inspector to choose a new keeper. He was a brisk man with a waxed moustache and a list of requirements, and the first requirement was that the keeper be a grown man. He looked at Mira's bandaged hands and at the logbook, where Elias had written a full account of the storm in his large, shaky letters.

The inspector read the account twice. Mira took that as a good sign, because he did not look like a man who read anything twice.

He could not make a fourteen-year-old girl the official keeper; the rules would not allow it. But he appointed Elias as keeper for five more years, with Mira Okafor listed as assistant keeper, at full pay, and he arranged for a doctor in Port Saltry to examine Elias's eyes. "In five years," he said, "we will talk about who keeps this light."

That night Mira opened a new logbook. She wrote the weather, the ships that passed, and the time the lamp was trimmed. At the bottom she signed the entry M.O., in neat, slanted letters, and below it she added one line that was not about the weather at all: "Still here. Will stay."

The light flashed twice every ten seconds, and far out on the dark water, the ships knew exactly where they were.
