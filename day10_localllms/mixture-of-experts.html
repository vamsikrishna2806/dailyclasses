Mixture of Experts (MoE), explained simply

Some of the biggest AI models today, like DeepSeek-V3, Mixtral, Qwen3-30B-A3B and gpt-oss, are "Mixture of Experts" models. They have a huge brain, but each word only wakes up a small part of it. This page shows how that works.

MoE = many specialists + a receptionist who picks the right few for each word.
Start with a story

🏥 The hospital

Picture two hospitals.

Hospital A: one super-doctor

One doctor knows everything. Every patient, whether they have a broken leg, a toothache or a fever, is seen by that same huge brain, and the whole brain has to think about every case.

Smart, but slow and expensive.

Hospital B: specialists + a receptionist

There are 8 specialist doctors. A receptionist looks at each patient and sends them to the 2 best doctors. The other 6 rest.

Same total knowledge, much less work per patient.

The definition

So what is Mixture of Experts?

A Mixture of Experts model splits part of its brain into many smaller networks called experts. A tiny network called the router (or "gate") decides which few experts should handle each word.

Three words to remember

WordSimple meaningHospital version
ExpertA small neural network: one of manyA specialist doctor
RouterA tiny network that scores the experts and picks the best fewThe receptionist
Top-kHow many experts are picked for each word (often 2 to 8)"See the 2 best doctors"

Step by step, for every word

  1. A word (token) arrives as a list of numbers.
  2. The router gives every expert a score: "how useful are you for this word?"
  3. It picks the top-k experts (say, the top 2).
  4. Only those experts do their math. All the others are skipped.
  5. Their answers are mixed together, weighted by their scores. That mixing is where the name "Mixture" comes from.

A little deeper

Where the experts live inside the model

An LLM is a stack of layers (for example, 32). Each layer has two main parts:

  1. Attention: "Which earlier words should I look at?" This part is shared; every word uses it.
  2. Feed-forward network (FFN): "What do I know about this?" This is the biggest part of the model. In MoE, this part is replaced by many experts + a router.
Dense layer:   word → Attention → [ one big FFN ]                    → next layer
MoE layer:     word → Attention → Router → [E1] [E2] [E3] ... [E8]  → mix → next layer
                                    picks top-2 ↑    ↑
Important: the router picks experts again for every word and in every layer. The word "cat" might use experts 2 and 5 in layer 1, then experts 1 and 7 in layer 2. A whole sentence ends up touching lots of different experts.

The tiny bit of math (optional)

# router gives a score to each of 8 experts, then softmax turns scores into %
scores = [0.1, 0.6, 0.0, 0.05, 0.2, 0.0, 0.03, 0.02]
top-2  = expert 2 (0.6) and expert 5 (0.2)
weights after re-scaling: 0.6/0.8 = 75%,  0.2/0.8 = 25%
output = 0.75 × Expert2(word) + 0.25 × Expert5(word)

Connect to CPU / GPU / RAM / VRAM

What MoE means for your computer

From the hardware guide: speed ≈ memory bandwidth ÷ bytes read per word. MoE changes the "bytes read per word" part.

📦 Memory: needs the FULL size

Any word might need any expert, so every expert must be loaded in RAM or VRAM. Qwen3-30B-A3B at 4-bit is about 18–19 GB, the same as a dense 30B model.

⚡ Speed: like the ACTIVE size

Each word reads only the active experts: about 3B parameters, roughly 2 GB. So it writes about as fast as a 3B model, even on a CPU.

# rough example on a CPU with ~60 GB/s RAM bandwidth (4-bit)
Dense 32B   reads ~19 GB per word  →  60 ÷ 19 ≈ 3 words/s    🐢
MoE 30B-A3B reads  ~2 GB per word  →  60 ÷ 2  ≈ 30 words/s (max)   🚀  real-world maybe 15–25
Why this is great for local LLMs: many laptops have lots of RAM (32 GB) but little or no VRAM. An MoE model fits in that RAM, and because only a small part is read per word, it still runs at a usable speed on the CPU.

Class experiment

  1. Run a dense model of similar size, e.g. ollama run qwen2.5:32b --verbose, and note the eval rate.
  2. Run ollama run qwen3:30b-a3b --verbose with the same question and note the eval rate.
  3. Compare with ollama ps: similar memory, but the MoE model writes many times faster.

Clear the confusion

Myths vs reality

MythReality
"Each expert is a topic expert: a math expert, a history expert…"Not really. Researchers find experts specialize in patterns such as punctuation, certain word types or code syntax, not neat school subjects. Nobody assigns topics; the specialization emerges during training.
"MoE needs less memory."No. All experts must be loaded. MoE saves compute and speed, not memory.
"A 47B MoE is as smart as a 47B dense model."Usually it lands somewhere in between: smarter than a dense model of its active size, but often a little below a dense model of its total size.
"The router picks experts once per question."It picks per word, per layer, thousands of times in one answer.
"Mixtral 8x7B = 8 separate 7B models glued together."No. Only the FFN parts are separate experts. Attention and embeddings are shared, so the total is 46.7B, not 56B.

Why training MoE is tricky (one-line version)

If the router falls in love with one or two experts, they get all the work and the others never learn. Training adds a "load balancing" rule that rewards spreading words fairly across experts, like a receptionist told not to send everyone to the same doctor.
